# The Technology That Centers You
subtitle: A conversation with Jack Wagoner
source: grateful

[note] Steven was a guest on *The Grateful Podcast*, hosted by Jack Wagoner, in an episode published June 11, 2025. Jack introduced him as the founder of Impossible Laboratories and the maker of Threads, a wearable first meant for people with ADHD. What follows is a selection from their conversation.

@B0007 Steven

## Built for myself

@B0010 Jack
So Threads, I believe that was your first piece of technology, right?

@B0010 Steven
It's the first one that I have released that isn't like a prototype or a piece of test software.

@B0010 Jack
And that was just a version, like you said, of something you'd built for yourself to help with your attention and productivity. Is that correct?

@B0011 Steven
Yeah, it's exactly that. I built pretty much all of this for myself. That's what motivates me. It's very hard to get excited about a problem that I don't share. But honestly, I was trying to build something much more complicated and I was frustrated and like, "This is what I need. I need something to help me manage my working memory, because I can't hold all these things in my head just over the span of a day. I don't need a to-do list to know what to do next week. I need something that just helps me know what I'm supposed to be doing right now, maybe what's next, and just manage the actual process of in-the-moment focus."

@B0012 Steven
It's not even really about productivity so much as it is I need to get stuff out of my head so I can do one thing. Because if I have to remember that I need to do that, or somebody's interrupted me, or I interrupt myself, I just lose a train of thought. So I needed a very simple system to just hold on to, almost like sticky notes. "Here's what I'm doing right now. Here's what I'm doing next." To manage that process. And that's all it is.

@B0013 Jack
So the idea is you're writing stuff down to empty your head of thinking about it so that you can fully focus on the task that you want to tackle. Is that correct?

@B0013 Steven
Yeah, there's a lot of potential uses, but it is essentially a way to extend your short-term and working memory in a way that doesn't get buried. You can do this stuff with pen and paper and reminders and alarms and Pomodoro. Those things get garbage collected, first of all, especially by people with ADHD, because of low object permanence. That you can't see it, it stops existing. Literally, it's not that you can't remember, it's that it no longer exists in your mind until you see some indication, "Oh, that thing exists." And so because neurodivergent people tend to live in the environment they're in, that's all that exists.

@B0014 Steven
You go off on a tangent and you never come back, or you become overwhelmed and burdened, literally stressed out by the number of things you're trying to remember, what you're supposed to be doing, and it degrades everything you do. It feels bad. "Oh, I'll just check my e-mail real quick. And oh, that looks interesting. I want to get to that later today." But I don't want to hold on to it. I don't want to remember it. And that's what we do, is we try to hold these things in our head and then we end up dropping them. We forget that we're supposed to do something and it becomes too difficult.

## The other edge

@B0031 Steven
I think that it helps with reflection. It is the source of all of my ability to reflect and improve and be anti-fragile and understand what behavior, influences, caused me pain or problems, or are working, or making me feel better or worse. ## An active thing

@B0037 Steven
I would say it's a bigger — it's hard to say because it's different — bigger effect than actual ADHD medication has been on my life. It completely changes the way you see the world. I started out, I think, as you mentioned, like you or many others, where it's a sporadic habit. It's like, "I should eat healthy," or, "I should fix this part. I should do this thing." @B0038 Steven

You could understand consciousness in the world and what is happening just by sitting there, or have transcendental experiences, experience things that there's no other way to experience. @B0039 Steven
But the level of clarity and just patience, the ability to not immediately react, which is incredibly valuable when you're an impulsive person, goes up significantly. There's a half-life, of course, doesn't last forever. It allows you to step outside of your own emotions and thoughts, not only calm them down and quiet them down to 0 when you get good at it, but it allows you to step behind them and carry that forward into everyday life in such a way that, I don't know, you don't take it too seriously when you start to feel depressed or panicked or worried or angry. It's like watching somebody else be angry. You still feel it, but you don't take it too seriously because it doesn't feel like you anymore. So that's what it's done, in a nutshell.

## The chair test

@B0047 Jack
How did you start out? I have many friends, some with ADHD, that have trouble being quiet and not having any stimuli. So how did you get over that initial hump?

@B0048 Steven
Yeah, attention, much like productivity, is sort of like a distraction. It's really like a downstream effect, that attention gets destroyed. It really does have more to do with your level of stimulation need, kind of like not paying down a debt. The longer you go without paying it down, the worse it feels. So a lot of people are to a point where they can't fall asleep without music or show playing in their ears or watching it, or they can't not have sound playing. They can't do a chore without sound playing. This comes from basically a low-level baseline dopamine and a bunch of other neurological changes. You can't sit still.

@B0049 Steven
So one of the things I recommend to people, I wrote this down so I wouldn't forget the name, is the chair test, which is relevant. It is basically, if you can't sit in a chair for 5 minutes and do nothing or just look at a wall, you are not OK. And that is a quick heuristic that I use in my life. If I can't do that or I am resisting doing that, that alone is a sign that I'm not OK on the inside, and anything I do on the outside is only going to make it worse or to kick the problem down the road.

@B0050 Steven
So there's an answer how to get over it, but it changes the framing to "I'm not OK." But you will rebalance your pleasure-pain systems. That's from *Dopamine Nation*, the book. You will rebalance the systems literally just by letting yourself sit there. So if you will just sit there in a chair for probably about 5 minutes, maybe longer depending on how long it's been, and just feel the pain — kind of like an ice bath or something, it is good pain — you will find that it subsides and then you're just mildly bored or OK, however long you have to sit there, and that the longer you put it off, you're actually making it worse and you're also depriving yourself of actual pleasure.

@B0051 Steven
It's like the 50th of ice cream. You don't really even feel it anymore. If you would like to actually taste it again, you have to stop eating ice cream for a little while and just feel terrible. Just crash. You just have to crash. You should think of pleasure as being conserved. There's always the same amount, and the more you try to pile on more pleasure, you're actually just devaluing all the pleasure to zero. You don't taste it anymore. So you're going to get the same amount regardless whether you're a monk who gets no ice cream or somebody who's just eating more and more ice cream, trying to feel more and more pleasure but feeling nothing.

@B0053 Jack
Right, at some point it zeroes out. At some point there is no additional pleasure, or there's negative.

@B0053 Steven
Yeah, that is unfortunately what happens. Why you're not OK sitting in a chair is because your brain rebalances towards pain by default, because your midpoint basically is below the sort of 0, OK line. To just turn it into a metaphor, that's a rough model for how it works, is if you are feeling too much pleasurable stimulation for too long, or comfort, that's probably a better word for it, then you will feel bad by default doing nothing, so you'll be in the negatives. But you will go back to 0 if you just sit there and feel the pain. But you go back to 0 even faster if you deliberately become uncomfortable.

## The balance point

@B0054 Jack
Are you familiar with economic utility theory?

@B0054 Steven
No, but the words alone sort of describe what it is, that everything has some economic utility. Or what does it mean?

@B0054 Jack
In economics, there's this concept of utility. It's basically how much you get out of something, like how much pleasure.

@B0055 Jack
As you start getting more and more of something, there's this thing called marginal utility, which is the pleasure you get with an additional unit of that thing. Eventually you get diminishing returns. So you're getting less and less on every additional unit, and then at some point, you start just going down.

@B0057 Steven
Here's a useful example. I think that it is exactly what you're talking about, but incorporates also a balance point, which is, your average level of stimulation is a descriptor of how you're going to feel when nothing's happening. How are you when nothing is happening? That's what your balance point is, more or less. Why is it that after running a marathon you would be happy to just lay down in mud? "That would be amazing. I would just love to just lay down. That would be so great." But if you're just walking outside and maybe going to the park and you lay down in mud, it's intolerable. "This is terrible."

@B0058 Steven
And the difference is, you've been through an immense amount of pain, and so literally doing nothing is complete ecstasy. "This is awesome. I would love to just lay here. You got 1/2-eaten sandwich? I'll knock the ants off. I'll take that. That would be amazing. I would love that." But in a different situation, that's intolerable pain. The only difference really is what you've experienced lately, but there are long-term trends as well that take time to work through.

@B0059 Steven
If you're used to comfort and stimulation and pleasure constantly, that is what it takes for you to feel normal, not to feel happy. And so you will feel bad by default, which is why it's so hard to just sit there. But it will stop happening if you just change your balance point by changing what you do. Just deal with the pain for a little while, it'll go away.

## Having both

@B0069 Steven
That is incredibly prescient for your age. It wants to create things outside of yourself and impact. And you can shorthand this as ambition, but it is a desire to build, not just to explore, to go deep on something that takes a long time, difficult or challenging, no one has asked you to do.

@B0070 Steven
And these things, you just sort of approach it without any thought, they'll destroy each other. I mean, look at pretty much every rich or successful artist, entrepreneur. They're almost universally unhappy. They're very sick people. And it is their obsession that made them great at what they do. It just sort of destroyed the quality of their life. Is that good?

@B0070 Steven
And then you have, you know, like the yogis or whatever. If you want to feel completely enlightened and just amazing all the time, I mean, the playbook is there. They tell you how to do it. @B0072 Steven
But I see both as being valuable. And I want elements of both. But I sort of insist on having both without them destroying each other. And that has been a principal challenge of my life. @B0073 Steven
So while people are telling you that their marriage is falling apart because they can't stop looking at a website, they have pornography addictions or drug addictions, it's difficult to stare those problems in the face, or your own insecurities or your own desires, and maintain a sort of calm, peaceful, happy demeanor and not just get wrapped up in what you're doing and all sorts of unhealthy ways. So it is exceptionally challenging. I'd say it's a noble goal. I don't have that one all figured out, but I have found strategies that I can do it for a while.

@B0074 Steven
It's hard to maintain that sort of state. In my mind at least, my ideal of perfection for this duality is this sort of highly accomplished, creative, innovative person who's completely unflappable. I want to be invincible. I want to go forward and do incredible things with other people, and I don't want to care about it at all. I don't want to need any of it. ## The center of your life

@B0075 Jack
I'm so in alignment with that. You don't want your pursuit of a goal to trap you in that pursuit, right? You don't want to be so committed to an outcome of something that you can't listen to what you're telling yourself.

@B0076 Steven
