# Human and Machine Consciousness
subtitle: A conversation with Ryan Estes
source: unlocking

[note] This conversation was recorded for the podcast AI for Founders, hosted by Ryan Estes. In his introduction Ryan describes Steven as the founder of Impossible Labs, a software engineer with over 1,000 hours of meditation practice.

## An hour every morning

@B0003 Ryan
All right, Steven, how are you doing?

@B0004 Steven
I'm having a pretty good day, actually. Just finished a run, so feeling pretty mellow. Did a long meditation. Ready to chat.

@B0005 Ryan
When you became available for an interview, one thing really stood out, and that's this: the intersection of AI and human meaning, meditation research, brain-computer interfaces, and sensory substitution. So because you mentioned meditation, let's start there. What kind of meditation do you do?

@B0006 Steven
At this point, I have my own spin on it. I don't follow a specific school or methodology, although I like to borrow from them. Frankly, I do about an hour of meditation every morning. I've probably got maybe 1,000 hours under my belt, so I'm not a guru or anything, but for me, it's two different things. This is how I reset myself. This is how I get my brain and body to function. It has a bigger effect than almost anything else I do in life, even than medications, anything like that, besides maybe sleep.

@B0008 Steven
And the other is inner space exploration. It's that we've got this tool to explore what is reality, right? How does consciousness interact with the physical world, and what are the rules? And so you got this tool to probe at what are the rules of experience. And I can't think of a better tool for exploration for meaning and purpose and our place in the world, what matters, how it all works. So that's the other big thing. That's what it is to me.

@B0010 Ryan
So you've got 1,000 hours under your belt. What brought you to the cushion?

@B0011 Steven
Well, I started out probably like a lot of people. It's, "Okay, I'll do 10 minutes here, 20 minutes here. I'll guilt trip myself into it. I'll take a couple months off." It was a thing you should do, you know? And then at some point, I read the autobiography from Michael Singer. I think it's *The Surrender Experiment*. Then I read his actual book after that. The name escapes me at this point. It'll come back. *The Untethered Soul*.

@B0012 Steven
And he talked about just, "Oh, I did this two-hour meditation and had this profound experience." And I'm like, "What? You can do that just by sitting there?" I had no idea that this was even a thing. Very naïve at this point in time. Didn't know anything about yoga traditions or the yogis or, you know, there's paths to enlightenment or different techniques or any of the altered states that are possible without psychedelics or anything, right? No intervention at all.

@B0013 Steven
The things you can do with just your own mind and where you put your attention and your concentration is crazy, and I had no idea you could reach these kinds of experiences or insight just by sitting there properly, and that's really what brought me in is this fascination. It's the same thing that draws me to technology. It's like, I gotta know. I gotta play with it. I'm curious. I need to know the rules, and can I do some engineering with it once I understand how it works? So, yeah, that's what pulled me in.

## That's not me

@B0015 Ryan
And then when you're sitting, you said you're pulling from multiple traditions, but nothing in particular. What are you doing when you're meditating?

@B0016 Steven
Sometimes it's, oh, I realize I'm angry and stressed out, and most of the session is just resetting to zero, right? That's not exciting. My basic protocol draws from the Yoga Sutras, right? It's pretty simple. It is a process of letting go, attempting to find a center, and basically whatever you're putting your attention on is not the thing to put your attention on.

@B0016 Steven
And it is a process of progressively relaxing and moving your attention almost like a knot. You just continue to invert whatever you're looking at. It's basically moving from big, overt bodily sensations to, okay, now emotional. Now I'm aware of thoughts. It's a continual separation of, oh, that's not me. That's not me. That's not me, until there's nothing left but the sense of self, and then if you want to, you can try to keep going and have an ego death experience.

@B0017 Steven
That's, frankly, not a realistic goal for most sessions. I really don't enjoy it. It's not a fun experience. But sometimes I'll pick other targets for meditation depending on what I'm trying to do, but that's my go-to.

## Irked by dogma

@B0019 Ryan
And why not choose a particular path? Why not zazen? Why not a mindfulness-based stress reduction? Why not Dzogchen? Is there something you're gaining by not committing to a particular dogma?

@B0020 Steven
I am totally open to being naive, and probably a lot of these practitioners would be happy to tell me that. Something in me just is irked by dogma and authority and tradition, and "this is the way it's done." I love to just come in, slurp up, "Okay, why does it work that way? How does it function? Let me try it myself." And I wanna try to understand why the existing structures work in anything, really.

@B0022 Steven
And then I take it, and I leave the rest 'cause I'm completely uninterested in, "Well, we do this because it's the way. This is the next step. You don't get to progress. Now you gotta study these stupid koans for a long time." I don't think I'm gonna do that. I'm gonna follow my interest and my intuition, and that's what I'm gonna do.

@B0023 Steven
I think that it can be detrimental, this mindset in life, because it causes you to, if you're not aware of what you're doing, to waste a bunch of cycles replicating work that other people have already figured out. However, at some point, it's also where everything new comes from, are from people who are interested in defying what already exists, or reassembling or synthesizing from multiple sources. And that's, frankly, what draws me, is I wanna learn it. I wanna do it myself, make it my own, or I'll just quit because it's boring.

## Strapping yourself to a rocket

@B0043 Ryan
What was your experience with psychedelics, and how does that incorporate in any way to your sitting practice?

@B0044 Steven
Pretty limited. So pretty much legal forms of marijuana and psilocybin. So they're all not nearly as strong as the illicit compounds. But enough to have a profoundly different experience, all right? We'll put it that way. But I did it after having done a lot of meditation and found that, to my great surprise, oh, there's nothing really new here.

@B0048 Steven
Now, I'm sure this is different if you're using DMT or something, you're using that, what is it? That cactus or flower compound out in the middle of a desert. I'm sure that's a different experience entirely. But for the most part, I was shocked to find, oh, this is the same thing. I've been here, right? This is next door.

@B0050 Steven
And so I described it to others as, okay, psychedelics is like strapping yourself to a rocket. You got no choice. You're on the ride. Or you could learn how to fly, and you can go as slow as you want with an airplane. You could just putter around on the runway. You could take off whenever you're ready. You decide you don't like it, you can land again. So it's a useful thing, and probably better most of the time. But sometimes, some people, and I guess on purpose, you need a rocket launch to really kick yourself out of where you are.

## Innermost intention

@B0063 Ryan
So meditation, you're doing an hour a day, which is quite a bit. When do you like to do meditation? And talk a little bit how it influences your life.

@B0066 Steven
Well, I didn't start that way, right? I started slowly increasing. But I noticed I was getting to more and more insight. It was profoundly affecting me. At certain times, I was scrambling to this as a way to find insight. The easiest way of describing it is it allows me to insulate myself from the world and to—there's a half-life to it—but bring to my work, to my family interactions, everything I do, to act from as innermost intention as you can have, right?

@B0067 Steven
I'm trying to make sure that the first and most important intentions I have for the day or my time, or even in response to things that I didn't expect, are coming from my values or from my desires and not what the world has pushed into my field of view. So it has been an amazing tool for that.

@B0067 Steven
So I found that the more I increased the time, the better effects I got. And then at some point, just like long-distance running, you connect the loop, the reward circuitry, and like, "Oh, this is helping me." And it no longer became a challenge, most of the time, to sit down and do that time.

@B0068 Steven
I don't do it right as I wake up because I'm too tired, and I'm cranky or sort of foggy-headed. I usually do it a little later, between 8:00 and 10:00 AM. It's about two hours after waking or three or four hours after waking. But yeah, so that's my schedule, and that's a lot of what it's for. And I often will just pick what I'm interested in, and I will pick a technique or a target of focus and just try stuff out. There's definitely more to the story than that, but that's my schedule.

@B0083 Steven

@B0084 Steven

## The science comes first

@B0129 Ryan
Now there is kind of an emergent understanding within, we'll just call them atheists, that say, "No, what's happening with human consciousness isn't necessarily limited to a substrate." Rather, there might be a particle of consciousness. There might be a gravity type field of consciousness that once you reach a certain complexity that you can tap into. And so basically what they're doing is taking away the god and goddess realm, which I don't know if that's necessary to do that. They take away all the magic, and they're like, "No, consciousness is an emergent phenomenon inside the universe."

@B0130 Ryan
If consciousness is not limited by substrate and in fact is just an emergent factor of the universe, do you think that we'll be able to create an AGI that can tap into that, become self-aware?

@B0131 Steven
That's a profound question. I guess my answer to these camps is yes, and don't be so quick to assume that either view is right or either view is wrong. My take is, at this point in time, I don't know. But I'm trying to find out. That is my take. I have hunches, I have ideas, but I hold them all loosely or as loosely as I can, but I have to have some beliefs in order to progress and test things. But I've a position of complete humility.

@B0132 Steven
I believe that there is a lot to learn from what has existed and what people have already studied, and it would be silly to throw away thousands of years of knowledge and practice. It's an amazing starting point. At the same time, I also think that we should be open to it being weirder than we think. It's premature to start closing off too many things when really we've only just started exploring with new tools of science, technology, and engineering and bringing those to bear, and to some degree logic and mathematics and physics, and bringing those tools to bear as well, and they are equally valid. And I'm not interested in anyone who thinks we should throw away any of these tools.

@B0133 Steven
Now, that said, using AGI, well, I think we need to learn a lot more about consciousness, especially as it applies to the ethics of artificial intelligence. If we are running these things in a box at a million times the cycle rate of your brain, what are we doing to them? Enough people have seen *Black Mirror* at this point to understand the implications that we should be careful because we don't know what's conscious. We don't know what is in there experiencing things. And for that reason alone... Can it be done? Maybe.

@B0134 Steven
We need to understand the interface of how human consciousness interfaces with the material world. Are they separate? How are they separate? How are they connected? What are the rules? Why are they connected this way? Why is it limited to these perspectives? We need to understand how it works, and then from there we can begin to, just like any found piece of technology—the human brain and body is a found piece of technology really. We can, just like we've done with AI up until this point, begin to reverse engineer the consciousness connection to the body and understand why it is we experience what we experience and how attention and all these things correlate.

@B0135 Steven
And being open to very different rules of reality than we thought existed, then we can start engineering with it. But I think it's premature to engineer when we don't even have the science. The science comes first, and I don't mean you have to have an academic paper and a lab and do these things. Doing personal exploration is valid science. There's lots of different kinds of evidence. But you need to have some basis, some priors in order to begin engineering, and that's really what I think we need to understand. What are the ground rules of the game? Let's start testing stuff and taking it seriously.

@B0136 Steven
So that is my point. I think it could be possible, but I, at least, don't have a high confidence that we know enough of the rules with enough confidence to begin engineering something reliable or even understanding what it's doing.

## What it's aware of

@B0140 Steven

@B0141 Steven

@B0142 Steven
I would have to guess it's a lot like being a sort of mycelial network, or a tree root network of a lot of different organisms, but in fast-forward and all at the same time from one perspective. Like, it's all one solid thing rather than multiple things. I think that that is what it would be, the closest experience. Now, what is that like? Tell me.
