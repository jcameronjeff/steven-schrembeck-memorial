### The same instructions as one sequence (editor's convenience; my paraphrase, not his words)

1. Be on steady ground first, and do not do this alone (groups 1 and 8).
2. Get quiet. A daydreamy, nearly asleep state is the one he recommends most (group 1).
3. Bring to mind the feeling of the person, which he calls the soul tone, and intend to speak to them (group 2).
4. Ask permission, say what you want to say, and be polite. Sending is the easy half (groups 2 and 3).
5. Wait without forcing. Notice thoughts, feelings, images or a sense of presence that arrive unbidden or out of context (group 4).
6. Treat what arrives as real for the purpose of the conversation, and hold it lightly afterward (groups 4 and 7).
7. Check it: ask again, use the feeling in the chest, switch method, come back later. Assume most of it is you (group 5).
8. Expect your own fears and wishes to write some of the lines, especially on subjects you care about most (group 6).
9. Ask for something that can be checked, that you do not already know, and that allows partial credit (group 7).
10. Stop if it frightens you or makes your life worse (group 8).

## Strongest parallels

1. **Contact at the edge of sleep and in dreams.** Steven: "If you can almost fall asleep, like taking a nap, that is when you get the clearest communication" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0100); and of his dead father's voice in a dream, "I know that it wasn't a dream. It was him." (`04-see-wake-up-remember-2.txt`, B0165). In a large recent survey of people reporting after-death communication, sleep was the most common setting (62.4% of 991), and the authors write that "ADCs tend to occur during hypnagogic and hypnopompic periods as well as during dreams" (elsaesser2021). In a hospice survey, 58% of 278 bereaved people had dreamed of the person who died (wright2014). Closeness: close. Difference: the surveys describe unsought experiences of the bereaved, while Steven teaches a deliberate practice; and the half-asleep state is also where sleep science expects the mind to produce voices and images by itself (hypnagogia).

2. **A presence recognized without sight or sound.** Steven: "you just feel a presence, not necessarily in the room, but in your mind, in yourself" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0049). In the landmark early study of widowed people, simply feeling the presence of the dead spouse was the most common experience, reported by 39.2% of 293 (rees1971). In a 2022 analysis, 34% of 991 cases were a sense of presence with no input from the five senses, and such presences were "identifiable as a specific deceased presence despite the lack of sensory cues" (woollacott2022). Closeness: close. Difference: Steven turns that recognition into an address that can be dialed ("I call it a soul tone.", B0044); the surveys record it only as something that happens to people.

3. **What the dead are reported to say.** After the mediums' session Steven found that "I kinda just wanted them to tell me that they loved me" (`01-prologue.txt`, B0313); the next morning's text was "something about grandmas and loving you" (B0322). Survey researchers sort the messages people report into four kinds, "reassuring", "resolving", "reaffirming" and "releasing", and gloss the third as "I love you, I will always be by your side, we'll meet again one day" (elsaesser2021). Closeness: close in content. Difference: the match says nothing about the source. Love and reassurance are what a grieving mind would most want to supply, which is the distortion Steven himself warns about (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0066 to B0068).

4. **Learning to tell which thoughts are not yours.** Steven: "You need to practice discernment. The one way to do that is through meditation." (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0071). The anthropologist T. M. Luhrmann studied American evangelical congregations whose members learn, through prayer practice, to hear God in their own inner experience; her team found that "The more they prayed, the more likely they were to have these experiences" (luhrmann2010). Closeness: close on method (sustained attention to inner events, regular practice, and learning to treat some of what arises as coming from another). Difference: the other party is God, not the dead or a soul family, and Luhrmann deliberately makes no claim about whether anyone is there.

5. **"You're not going to die, and everything loves you."** Steven's sign-off (`02-reality-101.txt`, B0298, and the last line of five chapters) matches two convictions commonly brought back from near-death experiences. In a prospective Dutch study of 344 cardiac-arrest survivors, 62 reported an experience; of those, 56% reported positive emotions and 32% meeting deceased persons, and at two and eight years they reported larger falls in fear of death, and larger gains in belief in an afterlife and in being loving toward others, than survivors who had no such experience (vanlommel2001). The main membership body in the field describes communication in these experiences as mind to mind, without spoken words (iands). Closeness: moderate. Difference: Steven does not describe being near death; his conviction came through meditation, liminal states and a remote-viewing session. The content converges, the circumstances do not, and near-death accounts have been common knowledge since 1975, so independence cannot be shown.

6. **Talking to the dead as an ordinary thing to do.** Steven: "I just say goodnight to everybody." (`04-see-wake-up-remember-2.txt`, B0166). In a 2023 national survey, 28% of US adults had told a dead relative about their life in the past year, 34% had felt a dead relative's presence, and 53% said they had at some time been visited by a dead family member in a dream or some other form (pew2023). Grief research since 1996 treats a continuing relationship with the dead as normal (klass1996). Closeness: close as behavior. Difference: none of this is evidence that the message is received.

7. **Insisting on something that can be checked.** Steven: "And if it's true, it should be verifiable." (`00-invitation.txt`, B0004). Two researchers left exactly that kind of test: Robert Thouless published enciphered messages in 1948 and Ian Stevenson set a combination lock in the 1960s, each intending to send the key after death (thouless1948, stevenson1968). No medium has supplied a key; two of Thouless's ciphers were later broken by computer. Closeness: moderate. Difference: Steven said in advance that this kind of test would be the hardest, because "precision is difficult for all the reasons we mentioned, and some I don't understand" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0091), and recommended "something that you can get partial credit on" (B0090). A skeptic will say that this moves the goalposts; a sympathetic reader will say it is an honest report of the limits he met.

8. **A reading by mediums that did not obviously hit.** Steven sat with five mediums in training ("So they stare at me for 20 minutes over Zoom.", `01-prologue.txt`, B0311) and reported "No dead ringers or anything." (B0313). Controlled studies are mixed: one research group reports above-chance accuracy under blinding (beischel2015), a carefully designed British test found none (okeeffe2005), and a meta-analysis finds a small positive effect (sarraf2021). Closeness: moderate. Difference: his session was informal and unblinded, so it can be compared with the research only as an experience, not as a test.

## Verdicts by area

| Area | Plain verdict |
|---|---|
| 1. Near-death experiences | Solid that they occur (roughly 10 to 20% of cardiac-arrest survivors in prospective studies) and that love, peace and loss of fear of death are typical. Not established that they show survival of death: no hidden target has been identified in the two AWARE studies. Physiological accounts are plausible and incomplete. |
| 2. Deathbed visions and end-of-life dreams | Solid that dying people commonly see dead relatives and are comforted. The classic surveys are methodologically weak (secondhand, low response). The hospice interview studies are sound but small. No evidential weight for survival beyond anecdote. |
| 3. After-death communication reported by the bereaved | Very solid that it is common (roughly a third to a half of bereaved people, more among the widowed) and usually comforting. Samples in the large recent surveys are self-selected. Claims of information the person could not have known rest on uncorroborated cases. |
| 4. Continuing bonds | Mainstream. The relationship continuing is normal. Whether it helps depends on the form it takes: in one large study, bonds expressed as illusions and hallucinations went with risk factors for complicated grief, while carrying the person inwardly went with growth. Reviews find sensed presence mostly benign. |
| 5. Deliberate methods | Weak to modest. Psychomanteum: about half report contact, grief eased across the whole group, no controls. Induced after-death communication: one observational comparison and one wait-list study, both from groups that work with the method. Dream incubation: fair evidence that asking shapes dream content. Letters and imagined conversations: part of tested grief therapies, with no claim of contact. Monroe's Lifeline: participant reports only. |
| 6. Research with mediums | Disputed. Small positive effects in blinded studies from a few committed laboratories; at least one well-designed null result; not accepted by mainstream psychology. |
| 7. Checkable tests | The tradition is honorable and the results so far are negative or unresolved. |
| 8. Risks | Solid: a minority of grief becomes a clinical condition; people who sell contact target the bereaved; wishful reading is the default, not the exception. |
| 9. Organizations | All four named in the assignment exist and do what they say. Details under "Open questions and leads". |

### Area 1. Near-death experiences

### moody1975

### greyson1983 and greyson2021
- Citation: Greyson, B. (1983). The near-death experience scale: construction, reliability, and validity. *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease*, 171(6), 369-375. Greyson, B. (2021). *After: A Doctor Explores What Near-Death Experiences Reveal about Life and Beyond*. St. Martin's Essentials.

### vanlommel2001
- Citation: van Lommel, P., van Wees, R., Meyers, V., & Elfferich, I. (2001). Near-death experience in survivors of cardiac arrest: a prospective study in the Netherlands. *The Lancet*, 358(9298), 2039-2045.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11755611 ; tables read in the reprint at https://www.anomalistik.de/images/pdf/sdm/sdm-2004-05-lommel-et-al.pdf
- What it reports: Of 344 consecutive resuscitated patients, 62 (18%) reported an experience. Among the 62: positive emotions 56%, awareness of being dead 50%, meeting deceased persons 32%, a tunnel 31%, communication with the light 23%. Occurrence was unrelated to duration of arrest, medication, or prior fear. At two and eight years the 62 reported less fear of death and more belief in an afterlife than the others. The authors argue: "With a purely physiological explanation such as cerebral anoxia for the experience, most patients who have been clinically dead should report one."
- Parallel: see Strongest parallels 5. Closeness: moderate.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: The best-known prospective study. Critics accept the data and dispute the inference: that only some patients report an experience may reflect differences in memory or brain state.

### parnia2014 and parnia2023 (the AWARE studies)
- Citation: Parnia, S., et al. (2014). AWARE: AWAreness during REsuscitation, a prospective study. *Resuscitation*, 85(12), 1799-1805. Parnia, S., et al. (2023). AWAreness during REsuscitation II. *Resuscitation*, 191, 109903.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25301715 ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37423492
- What they report: In the first, of 140 survivors interviewed after 2,060 arrests, 9% had a near-death experience and 2% described awareness of the resuscitation. "One had a verifiable period of conscious awareness during which time cerebral function was not expected." In the second, of 28 survivors interviewed after 567 arrests, 6 reported a transcendent experience, and brain activity consistent with consciousness was recorded up to an hour into resuscitation. Of the hidden test images: "Nobody identified the visual image".
- Parallel: The field's attempt at what Steven asked for, something checkable. Closeness: loose to his experiences, close to his standard.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: Careful studies with tiny surviving samples. The verified case concerned events and sounds of the resuscitation, not the hidden images. Both sides cite them.

### martial2025 and others (physiology cluster)
- Citation: Martial, C., et al. (2025). A neuroscientific model of near-death experiences. *Nature Reviews Neurology*, 21(6), 297-311. With nelson2006 (*Neurology*, 66(7), 1003-1009), klemencketis2010 (*Critical Care*, 14, R56), timmermann2018 (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 9, 1424) and xu2023 (*PNAS*, 120(19), e2216268120).
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40159547 ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16606911 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887177/ ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30174629 ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37126719
- What they report: The 2025 review proposes a cascade of psychological and neurophysiological processes under threat. Supporting findings: more sleep paralysis and sleep-onset hallucinations in people who have had these experiences; higher carbon dioxide in arrest survivors who report them; near-death-scale scores raised by the psychedelic DMT in 13 volunteers; a surge of organized gamma activity in two of four dying patients.
- Parallel: The state Steven recommends for contact, the edge of sleep, is the state these researchers link to near-death imagery. Closeness: moderate.

### Area 3. After-death communication reported by the bereaved

### rees1971
- Citation: Rees, W. D. (1971). The hallucinations of widowhood. *British Medical Journal*, 4(5778), 37-41.
- Confirmed at: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1799198/ ; breakdown read in kamp2020, Table 3
- What it reports: A Welsh general practitioner interviewed 227 widows and 66 widowers, about four in five of all the widowed people in his area. - Parallel: see Strongest parallels 2. Closeness: close.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.

- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.

### elsaesser2021 and woollacott2022
- Citation: Elsaesser, E., Roe, C. A., Cooper, C. E., & Lorimer, D. (2021). The phenomenology and impact of hallucinations concerning the deceased. *BJPsych Open*, 7(5), e148. Woollacott, M., et al. - What they report: An online survey in three languages; 991 usable responses, 85% from women. ### kamp2020
- Citation: Kamp, K. S., Steffen, E. M., Alderson-Day, B., et al. (2020). Sensory and quasi-sensory experiences of the deceased in bereavement: an interdisciplinary and integrative review. *Schizophrenia Bulletin*, 46(6), 1367-1381.
- Confirmed at: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7707065/ (full text read)
- What it reports: A review by a working group of the International Consortium for Hallucination Research. It concludes that "the vast majority of these experiences are benign", sets out five theories (trauma response, attachment and searching, continuing bonds, an internalized other in dialogue with the self, survival), and advises clinicians to welcome the disclosure, let it be told in detail, ask what it means to the person, say how common it is, and look further only if it distresses or other problems are present.
- Parallel: Its advice is close to Steven's stance: take it seriously, do not shut it down, decide for yourself what it means. Closeness: moderate. Difference: survival is one hypothesis of five and is not endorsed.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: The best single mainstream source for the chapter. It notes that voices in bereavement are "seldom anonymous", unlike many in psychosis, and that a significant minority of experiences are unwelcome. Two smaller studies agree: people benefit most when they have a framework that can hold the experience (steffen2011), and social workers of every private opinion handled disclosures by respecting the client's account (sanger2009).

- What it reports: A probability survey of 5,079 US adults. 53% said they had ever been visited by a dead family member, in a dream (46%) or another form (31%). - Parallel: see Strongest parallels 6. Closeness: close as behavior.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: The most representative figures available. It records what people say happened and asks nothing about cause.

### wright2014
- Citation: Wright, S. T., Kerr, C. W., et al. (2014). The impact of dreams of the deceased on bereavement: a survey of hospice caregivers. *American Journal of Hospice and Palliative Medicine*, 31(2), 132-138.

- What it reports: Of 278 bereaved people, 58% had dreamed of the person who died. Common themes: pleasant memories, the person free of illness or at peace, the person delivering a message. 60% said the dreams affected their grieving, mostly toward acceptance and comfort.

### Area 4. The relationship continuing

### klass1996
- Citation: Klass, D., Silverman, P. R., & Nickman, S. L. (Eds.). (1996). *Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief*. Taylor & Francis. Follow-up (klass2018): Klass, D., & Steffen, E. M. (Eds.). (2018). *Continuing Bonds in Bereavement*. Routledge.

- Parallel: Steven's goodnights (`04-see-wake-up-remember-2.txt`, B0166) and his view that those he speaks with are "just people" who care about you (`02-reality-101.txt`, B0062). ### field2010
- Citation: Field, N. P., & Filanosky, C. (2010). Continuing bonds, risk factors for complicated grief, and adjustment to bereavement. *Death Studies*, 34(1), 1-29.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24479173
- What it reports: In 502 bereaved people, bonds expressed as illusions and hallucinations of the dead went with violent death and with feeling responsible for it. Bonds expressed as carrying the person inside as a secure base went with fewer risk factors and with personal growth.

### apa-pgd
- Citation: American Psychiatric Association. Prolonged grief disorder (public information page).
- Confirmed at: https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder ; prevalence from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolonged_grief_disorder
- What it reports: The diagnosis entered the manual in March 2022. ### Area 5. Methods people have used deliberately

### moody1993
- Citation: Moody, R. A., with Perry, P. (1993). *Reunions: Visionary Encounters with Departed Loved Ones*. Villard. Earlier (moody1992): Moody, R. A. (1992). *Journal of Near-Death Studies*, 11(2).

- What it reports: Moody revived an ancient Greek practice: after talking at length about the dead person, the visitor sits in a dim, curtained room gazing into a mirror placed so that it does not show their reflection. Of about 300 people by 1993, about a quarter met someone other than the person sought, and for about a quarter the encounter came afterward, usually within a day.
- Parallel: Steven's method is the same recipe without the mirror: dwell on the person, relax into a drowsy state, do not force, wait. Closeness: moderate. Difference: Moody's subjects mostly saw; Steven mostly received thought.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: Moody's own reports, without controls or measurement, written for a popular audience. Mirror-gazing in dim light is said to produce visual illusions in ordinary volunteers; that literature is UNVERIFIED in this session.

### hastings2002
- Citation: Hastings, A., Hutton, M., Braud, W., et al. (2002). Psychomanteum research: experiences and effects on bereavement. *Omega: Journal of Death and Dying*, 45(3), 211-228.

### barrett1993 (dream incubation)
- Citation: Barrett, D. (1993). The "committee of sleep": a study of dream incubation for problem solving. *Dreaming*, 3(2).

### Area 6. Research with mediums

### beischel2015
- Citation: Beischel, J., Boccuzzi, M., Biuso, M., & Rock, A. J. (2015). Anomalous information reception by research mediums under blinded conditions II: replication and extension. *Explore*, 11(2), 136-142. Earlier (beischel2007): Beischel, J., & Schwartz, G. E. (2007). *Explore*, 3(1), 23-27. On grief (beischel2014): Beischel, J., Mosher, C., & Boccuzzi, M. (2014). *Omega*, 70(2), 169-194.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25666383 ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17234565 ; https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25628023
- What it reports: Twenty screened mediums gave 86 readings by phone, with mediums, raters and experimenters all blinded. In the 2007 design the person who wanted the reading was not on the call; an experimenter who knew nothing stood in. Each sitter scored two transcripts, their own and one meant for someone else, without knowing which was which. Target readings were rated more accurate and chosen more often than chance. The 2014 paper says that many bereaved people already seek readings and that little is known about the effect on grief.
- Parallel: This is what Steven's session with five mediums was not: blinded. His own reading left him sad at first (`01-prologue.txt`, B0313, B0318). Closeness: loose to his experience, close to his standard.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: Published in a journal friendly to such work; small numbers per analysis; run by researchers who think the effect is real. Partial independent support exists (kelly2011: one null study and one strongly positive; tressoldi2022). Battista, Gauvrit and LeBel argue that this literature suffers from flexible analysis and uneven samples (battista2015). The group's public guidance says a reading is rarely fully accurate and should be discussed with a mental health professional first if grief is the reason (windbridge).

### sarraf2021
- Citation: Sarraf, M., Woodley of Menie, M. A., & Tressoldi, P. (2021). Anomalous information reception by mediums: a meta-analysis of the scientific evidence. *Explore*, 17(5), 396-402.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32660815
- What it reports: Pooling 18 experiments from 14 papers (2001 to 2019), the authors estimate accuracy at 0.18 above chance on a standardized index, surviving two tests for publication bias. They conclude the evidence supports the hypothesis that "some mediums can retrieve information about deceased persons through unknown means".
- Parallel: none specific. Closeness: not rated.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: A small literature from a handful of laboratories, pooled across designs of uneven quality, in the journal that published most of the positive studies. "Unknown means" is careful wording: taken at face value the result still does not show that the dead are the source.

### okeeffe2005
- Citation: O'Keeffe, C., & Wiseman, R. (2005). Testing alleged mediumship: methods and results. *British Journal of Psychology*, 96(2), 165-179.
- Confirmed at: https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15969829
- What it reports: Two psychologists reviewed a century of methodological argument, designed a procedure to close the known loopholes (mediums isolated from sitters; every sitter rates every reading), and tested several professional mediums. "The results of this work did not support the existence of genuine mediumistic ability."
- Parallel: none specific. Closeness: not rated.
- Named by Steven or Joshua: no.
- Standing and criticism: The standard mainstream citation. Ray Hyman's critique of earlier positive work found weak precautions against sensory leakage and fraud, sitters predisposed to believe, and unsuitable statistics (hyman2003). The usual explanation of a convincing reading is cold reading: high-probability guesses, statements that fit most people, and attention to the sitter's reactions (coldreading).

### Area 7. Making contact checkable

### thouless1948
- Citation: Thouless, R. H. (1948). A test of survival. *Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research*, 48(175), 253-263. Solution (gillogly1996): Gillogly, J. J., & Harnisch, L. (1996). Cryptograms from the crypt. *Cryptologia*, 20(4), 326-329.

### stevenson1968
- Citation: Stevenson, I. (1968). The combination lock test for survival. *Journal of the American Society for Psychical Research*, 62, 246-254.
- Confirmed at: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Stevenson

### crosscorr
- Citation: Hamilton, T. (2017). Cross-correspondences. *Psi Encyclopedia*, Society for Psychical Research. Critique: Moreman, C. M. (2003), *Journal of the Society for Psychical Research*, 67.

## What is distinctive in Steven's account

- **An error budget.** He puts numbers on his own unreliability: assume "seventy to ninety percent of the information is just you talking to yourself" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0099); clear moments are "one in fifty" (B0098); words are the least reliable channel, feeling better, a bodily signal best (B0080 to B0085). I did not find a comparable self-audit in the sources above. Mediumship research measures accuracy from outside; practitioners rarely publish their own hit rate from inside.
- **The puppet.** His claim that practicing with an imagined version of someone is legitimate, and that "the puppet will eventually become more and more representative of the actual person you're trying to talk to" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0099). Grief therapy uses imagined conversations and the prayer practices Luhrmann studied make deliberate use of visualization, but the claim that the imagined figure converges on a real one is his.
- **The soul tone as an address.** Surveys report that a presence is recognized by its feel. Steven makes the feel the means of calling ("I call it a soul tone.", `07-telepathy.txt`, B0044). I did not find the term elsewhere; I could not search for it after the search allowance ran out, so treat this as provisional.
- **Etiquette.** "Ask permission because you are asking to share their mind, their conscious experience." (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0045). The idea that the other party may be busy, may decline, and should be treated politely is unusual in this literature, which mostly treats contact as something received.
- **A living author's invitation.** Thouless and Stevenson left tests. Steven left an invitation with a method attached and hedged both times ("give me a shout one day", `07-telepathy.txt`, B0106; "send me a message with your mind one day", `08-transcendence.txt`, B0106). I found no close precedent for a how-to recorded by the person the reader may later want to reach.
- **His own line.** He could accept unfamiliar presences but refused when his dead grandmother was brought forward: "deceased family members is a line for me" (`02-reality-101.txt`, B0124). That reluctance, in someone otherwise so willing, is worth the editor's notice.

## What a careful skeptic would say

A fair skeptic starts by granting the experiences. Sensing the dead is common, mostly benign, and not a symptom of illness (kamp2020, rees1971). The dispute is only about what produces it.

The ordinary account has four parts.

First, attachment. In the attachment account, losing someone we depend on sets off an urgent searching, and the person who is absent and longed for is, briefly, found. It is a short inference from there, and it is mine, that a person we love is also a set of long-practiced expectations, their step on the stair, their voice at a certain hour, which do not stop at the death. This explains why experiences are commoner after long, close relationships and in the first months (kamp2020), though the same review notes it does not explain experiences that come years later.

Second, the state. Steven recommends the edge of sleep, and that is where voices, names called, images and a loosened sense of self arise in most people without any outside party (hypnagogia). People prone to sleep-boundary intrusions are also those more likely to report near-death experiences (nelson2006, martial2025).

Third, training. Absorption and the belief that the mind is permeable predict who senses invisible others, and practice strengthens the effect (luhrmann2010, luhrmann2021). Steven's program, daily meditation, loosening ownership of one's thoughts, acting as though the other is real, resembles the kind of practice Luhrmann describes, and it would be expected to produce vivid, other-seeming thoughts whether or not anyone is sending them.

Fourth, checking. From the inside there is no mark on a thought that says where it came from; Steven says so himself (an invented line "feels exactly like the line before it", `07-telepathy.txt`, B0067). His remedies (ask again, consult the feeling in the chest: "It turns green or red.", `07-telepathy.txt`, B0085) use the same mind as the instrument, so they cannot be independent checks. Several of his rules make a miss impossible to count: of coincidences he says "You can't be wrong." (`08-transcendence.txt`, B0038); shuffled songs carry meaning "and it always does" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0010); answers need only be "directionally right" (B0092); and validation is scarce by design ("I don't get a lot of it because I think it's counterproductive.", `01-prologue.txt`, B0325). A method that cannot fail cannot confirm either. Where the outside world has been allowed to score the result, the record is thin: no hidden image identified in two resuscitation studies (parnia2014, parnia2023), no cipher key delivered (thouless1948), small disputed effects with mediums (sarraf2021, okeeffe2005).

On the grandmothers text, the skeptic would ask ordinary questions before extraordinary ones. The sender had trained alongside the mediums who gave the reading; did she know it had happened? A dead grandmother who sends love is about as likely a theme as there could be for a man of his age (a judgment of plausibility, not a statistic). And people remember the message that fits.

The skeptic should also say what this account does not do. It does not show that no one is there. It explains why the experience would occur either way, which removes the experience as evidence but leaves the question open. And it has nothing to say against the practice as a way of grieving: speaking to the dead is associated with comfort and is part of tested grief therapy (shear2005, elsaesser2021).

## Open questions and leads

### What a person in the editor's position could actually try

None of these comes with a promise. Each is listed with what it can and cannot show.

1. **Speak to him.** Aloud or in thought, in Steven's manner: bring the feeling of him to mind, ask, say it, be polite (groups 2 and 3 above). Can: continue the relationship, which is normal and usually consoling (pew2023, klass1996). Cannot: show that it is received.
2. **Ask at the edge of sleep, and write down what comes before doing anything else.** His own recommendation for practice (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0100). Can: make a dream or half-asleep experience of him more likely, since asking shapes dream content (barrett1993), and such experiences are often treasured (elsaesser2021). Cannot: tell a visit from a dream. Steven's own estimate is that most of what arrives is the receiver.
3. **Keep a dated log, including the nights when nothing happens and the impressions that turn out wrong.** Can: protect against remembering only the hits. Cannot: prove anything by itself.
4. **Ask for something checkable that you do not know and someone else can verify.** This is his own rule (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0090, B0092). Write the question and what would count as an answer before asking; have a third person hold the truth. Can: give a personal test that is able to fail. Cannot: rule out chance in a single case, and a miss, by his account of precision, would not count against him. Decide beforehand how much weight either outcome will bear.
5. **Find out whether he left a sign.** Ask those closest to him, and look in his journals and blog, for any phrase, object or signal he said he would use. Thouless and Stevenson show the idea is old. Can: turn an open-ended hope into a specific question. Cannot: be constructed after the fact; if he left nothing, that is the answer.
6. **Write to him, or speak to an empty chair.** Can: do what grief therapy uses these for, saying what was not said (shear2005, apa-pgd). Cannot: and does not claim to, bring a reply.
7. **If trying a medium, a psychomanteum or induced after-death communication, set it up so that it could fail.** See the next list. Budget in advance and do not exceed it; walk away from anyone who finds a problem that needs more sessions, asks for secrecy, or guarantees a result (aarp-scams). Can: produce an experience, sometimes a consoling one. Cannot: verify contact in one sitting. In the one measured psychomanteum study about half reported contact and grief eased across the whole group (hastings2002).
8. **Do it in company.** His advice (`08-transcendence.txt`, B0063, "Everybody needs somebody."). A second person also keeps the log honest.
9. **Know when to stop.** His rule: "don't do it or stop doing it" if it frightens you (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0105), and "there's no shame in calling it quits if it's making your life worse" (`08-transcendence.txt`, B0068). The clinical markers that grief itself needs help are, a year or more after the death, most days still marked by several of: intense emotional pain, numbness, a sense that life is meaningless, a feeling that part of oneself has died, and inability to re-engage with people and plans (apa-pgd). Thoughts of ending one's life are a reason to seek help at once, whatever the timing. His own warning about who should not attempt the practice at all, "especially for people with schizophrenia or other schizophrenic disorders" (`07-telepathy.txt`, B0036), stands.

### What a well-controlled sitting looks like

Drawn from beischel2007, beischel2015, okeeffe2005 and tressoldi2022:

- The medium has no contact with the person who wants the reading, and is given at most the dead person's name (tressoldi2022).
- A stand-in who knows nothing about the dead person takes the reading by phone, so nothing can leak through reactions.
- The reading is transcribed and broken into separate statements.
- The sitter scores two or more transcripts, their own and readings given for other people (matched at least for the dead person's sex), without knowing which is which.
- As ordinary good practice, the scoring rules and what will count as success are fixed beforehand.

A private person can approximate this: book under a first name only, say as little as possible, record it, then have a friend mix the statements with those from another person's reading before scoring. It will not be science, and it will be far better than the usual sitting.
