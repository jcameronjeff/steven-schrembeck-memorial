About this edition

Reality 101 is a podcast series Steven Schrembeck made with his friend Joshua Swenson. Its episodes were published between May and October 2026. Steven called it “a journal of impossible experiences”: his account of a run of experiences that began with UFO sightings in 2022, of what he came to believe those experiences showed about consciousness and reality, and of everything he knew about how another person could try the same things. Steven died in September 2026.

This book collects the whole series in the order he released it, followed by the three solo audio journals he added as bonus episodes, and then by selections from four interviews he gave in 2025, before the series began. It is meant for anyone who wants to read what happened to him and what he was trying to teach, and one day for his sons.

How it was made

Every sentence in the chapters was spoken by the person it is attributed to. The text was made from transcripts of the recordings by cutting only. Filler, stutters, false starts, repeated explanations, cross-talk, recording logistics and show promotion have been removed. Punctuation, capitalization and paragraph breaks are editorial. No word has been added, replaced or moved; where Steven says “gonna,” the page says “gonna.” A program checks each paragraph against the transcript, word by word and in order, every time the book is built.

Chapter titles follow the episode titles. Section headings are editorial. The few explanations added between passages are labelled *Editor’s context*. Joshua Swenson’s questions and reflections, and those of the interview hosts, appear under their own names, shortened by the same method.

The series is presented nearly complete: it keeps what Steven said and removes how speech stumbles. The interviews are selections, chosen for what they add. What was left out of each chapter is described under Sources and editorial note at the back.

What to keep in mind

The transcripts were made by machine and have not been fully checked against the audio by a listener. A machine can mishear a word, and a cut can change an emphasis. Where a transcribed word looked wrong, it was left as transcribed or the clause was left out; it was not replaced with a guess. The spelling of a small number of names was corrected. Anything that matters to you is worth hearing in his voice: every chapter’s recording and full transcript are in the Steven Schrembeck Archive, linked from the notes at the back.

This edition was prepared with AI assistance after Steven’s death. He did not review or approve it.

These are his accounts of his own experience. The book does not argue for them or against them, and it has not softened his doubts or his certainty. He asked for neither belief nor deference. “If it’s true,” he says in the trailer that opens this book, “it should be verifiable.”

How to read it

Start with the Invitation and the Prologue; he called the prologue required listening for everything after it. Parts One and Two then follow his own order. Part Three steps back to 2025, when he was describing the same search in its earlier stages, mostly to people who had asked him about technology.

A reader’s guide at the back gathers, in his words, the things he most wanted people to understand, the practices he describes for trying it yourself, and a list of the experiences he recounts, each linked to its place in the text.

Food and Frequency

An audio journal

And this came amidst a long series of serious and profound experiences. So this was a very welcome and very surprising experience. It starts as a dream, and I thought that the information in here was something I’d never heard before. It’s very practical in nature, and it’s kind of fun. So enjoy. Now, I’m gonna be reading from a journal for some of this, and I’ll interject my own thoughts. My own handwriting’s pretty hard to read, so if I’m stumbling through it, well, that’s why. All right, here we go.

The cooking show

I was a bodiless observer in a dream about a cooking show. Chefs had prepared one dish after another for a panel of judges. As it would turn out, the chefs, judges, and I were all actually students of a sort on this set, as it was a classroom in disguise.

A man presents a dish with some fish and leafy vegetables. One taster expresses satisfaction with the contrast between the fish and the vegetables. And then a Michael Pollan-like figure interjects. He’s bald with liver spots, and he starts to explain why the contrast is satisfying. “Look at how different the environments of the ingredients are.” Meaning the fish and the leafy greens came from very different environments. That’s why the contrast is satisfying.

Another taster, this is the judge on the panel, says something about the greens. And then, again, this figure interjects and says, “Your brain is desensitizing to the greens.” Meaning, to explain why they didn’t like it. So I’m in a dream, right? This is just happening. Another judge comments about something else on the plate. They make a comment about gluten. And then, again, the Michael Pollan-like figure interjects. He’s like the host, you know. He’s talking to the audience. He’s talking to the whole room, right? Every time somebody will present a dish, the judge says something, and then this guy starts to come in with, “Oh, I’m gonna layer some commentary over the top to explain some things.”

So they say, “You need to see beyond gluten-free. It is not that simple.” And with a flourish, the dream takes a turn and seems to become much more instructive. So they basically look right at the camera, and now they’re talking.

And I am seeing, intuitive, conceptual, visual, literally watching some grains grow from a seed in the field, and they’re being harvested or milled or whatever. They’re going through a distribution facility, prepared in a kitchen. I see this whole life cycle while this guy is talking this next quote. So it comes with a slideshow, a very fancy one. Nom

Back to the journal. Now I’m both curious and waking up. I’m becoming lucid. Before, during, or after

So he goes on to explain that a batch of Oreos could be made with love, and that you can tell the difference when something is made with love, and that there are three different ways to determine the frequency of a food as it relates to its health value for you. And you can do it before, during, or after eating something. So you can sense whether something is good for you before, during, or after eating it.

So the easiest time is after you’ve eaten it. It’s literally just how it makes you feel. That should be pretty obvious, right? I think everyone knows this one, like, “Ugh, I don’t feel good after eating that.” That’s the easiest. So these are each skills, and there is a level of awareness involved, and there’s some practice. But you can train yourself, and then during eating it, you can notice, “Ooh.” That one’s a little easier. And then the hardest, generally, is knowing whether food is healthy before you’ve eaten it. You can just sense it.

So they go on to say that every ingredient in the dish involved, the equipment, everything connected to each part matters separately. Everything is connected. Whether it was made on a stove that was well-loved or made on a stove that’s a piece of crap that somebody yelled at it every day because it never worked correctly. That makes a difference. So this guy’s blowing my mind at this point. I’m in a lesson, but I don’t even care. I’m less wowed at this point than receiving a telepathic lesson from a stranger. Frequency is contextual

So naturally, I’m gonna poke some holes in this. All right, so here’s my next question, question number two. So then I asked my next question. Is frequency not universal then? And I get yes and no simultaneously, at the same exact time. I thought they were very cool ideas, and I’d never heard frequency described in this way before, and that it’s all contextual. And I thought that was fascinating too. Sources and editorial note

This is a reading edition of fourteen recordings preserved in the Steven Schrembeck Archive. Thirteen were transcribed by machine with ElevenLabs Scribe v2 on October 10, 2026. The fourteenth, from The Grateful Podcast, uses the transcript its publisher supplied, which does not label speakers; speakers there were identified from context. No transcript has had a full listening review, and the source files are unchanged.

Each paragraph was checked by program against its transcript: its words must all occur, in the same order, in the transcript of the named speaker, starting at the place the manuscript cites. The check compares words only. Case, punctuation, quotation marks and paragraphing are editorial. Transcript spellings of five names were corrected: Steven, Schrembeck, Swenson, Estes, and Nom. The corrections are counted in the validation record. Matching words in order shows that nothing was added. It does not prove the machine heard each word correctly, and it does not make a selection neutral.

A companion file, the passage map, lists every paragraph with its recording, speaker, start and end times, the original transcript wording, and the edited wording. The manuscripts, the checker and the builder are kept with the archive so the edition can be rebuilt and re-verified.
