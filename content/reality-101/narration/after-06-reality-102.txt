## The one he had read

I'll start with the comparison that counts least as evidence, because it is the closest. In 1970 Jane Roberts published the first book of material she said was dictated through her by a personality called Seth. In it Seth tells three people looking at one glass that each of them is creating their own {cite:roberts1970}. That is very nearly the apple.

Steven knew the Seth books. He recommends them in the telepathy journal, and his own outline of this lecture, on a page of his site created April 9, 2026 and never finished, says "Full stop (shoutout to Seth)" {cite:schrembeck2026}.

So this is not two people arriving separately at the same place. It may be a man recognizing in his own experience something he had read. It may be a sleeping mind building a lecture out of its library. He gives one piece of evidence on the question himself, about the first lecture: {q:02-reality-101|Coming into this, I had not done a lot of study on esoteric stuff}. When he read what, I don't know. It matters, and his journals might settle it.

## The old problem

What the lecture is about, though, is a real problem and an old one. If each mind has its own world, why do we seem to share one?

Everyone who has proposed private worlds has had to add something that keeps them in step. For Leibniz, around 1714, each mind mirrors the whole universe from its own position, nothing passes between them, and God arranged the agreement in advance {cite:leibniz1714}. For the Buddhist philosopher Vasubandhu, some thirteen centuries earlier, shared experience comes from shared karma, and minds act on minds directly {cite:gold2021}. In Seth it is telepathy and mass belief. In the physicist Thomas Campbell's model, which Steven's vocabulary resembles and which he never mentions, a larger system computes one consistent world for everybody {cite:campbell2003}.

Steven's teacher refuses to add anything: {q:06-reality-102|There is no consensus mechanism. It is always your reality. You are the consensus mechanism.} I have not found that flat refusal anywhere else. Nor have I found the way the lecture keeps ordinary physics standing, through layers of yourself that stay faithful to the rules whatever your surface self would like.

## Layers

The idea that most of the self is out of view is respectable. Frederic Myers argued it in 1903, and hypnosis research in the 1970s described a "hidden observer" that registers what the hypnotized person does not feel {cite:myers1903,hilgard1977}. A current philosopher, Bernardo Kastrup, models each of us as a dissociated part of one mind {cite:kastrup2018}. All of these stop at the person. Steven's layers include the cup and the desk.

## Three cautions

Physics first. You will see experiments on "Wigner's friend" cited for claims like this one. They are real experiments. The observers in them were photons, and what they show is that a particular set of assumptions cannot all be true together {cite:proietti2019,bong2020}. They do not show that people live in separate realities. Steven does not cite them, to his credit.

Then blame. "The reason that anything happens to you is because you allow it" is a sentence that can be turned on anyone who has suffered. He sees that coming and refuses it: {q:06-reality-102|I've never liked the rhetoric that, oh, your kid got murdered or run over by a car because you allowed it. That's not what we're talking about.} If you quote the first sentence, quote the second.

Then testing. A model in which every rule is soft, doubt switches things off, and two contradictory outcomes both happen is hard to prove wrong. He knows it, and he keeps a ledger. Of the main lecture he says he is {q:06-reality-102|personally certain about it}. The follow-up, about what becomes of the other person, he holds {q:06-reality-102|with much lower confidence}. And of that follow-up he says: {q:06-reality-102|I don't like it.}

## What I wonder

I trust the ledger more than I trust the lecture. Someone making this up to impress would not tell you which part he suspects he got wrong.

And I notice the teacher's own disclaimer, which is the most engineer-like sentence in the book: {q:06-reality-102|It's correct, it's just not complete.}
