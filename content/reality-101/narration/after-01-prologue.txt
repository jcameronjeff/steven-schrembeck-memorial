## He is not the first engineer

The shape of this chapter, a careful materialist whose model breaks on one thing he can't explain, has been told often enough to have a name. The historian of religion Jeffrey Kripal calls it the flip and has collected cases of scientists and other skeptics it happened to {cite:kripal2019}. Federico Faggin, who designed the first commercial microprocessor, describes waking one night in 1990 into love and light, with no separation between himself and what he was seeing, and then spending twenty years trying to work out what had happened {cite:faggin2021}. Steven's line for the moment the model breaks is {q:01-prologue|What else am I wrong about, right?}

That it began with something in the sky is also common. The Harvard psychiatrist John Mack found that the people he studied were changed by their encounters, whatever the encounters were {cite:mack1994}. Kenneth Ring compared people who reported close encounters with people who had nearly died and argued that the after-effects were alike {cite:ring1992}. A survey of more than three thousand people who report contact found most of them describing it as non-physical, with reassuring messages {cite:hernandez2018}. That survey recruited its own respondents and had no comparison group, so it tells you what such people say. It does not tell you how often this happens or what it is.

## What the body did

The burning in his chest, the geometric visuals and the sounds, during long sittings in a closet, are the kind of thing a Brown University study recorded when it interviewed meditators about difficult experiences: energy in the body, lights, visions, heat, and a changed sense of where the self ends {cite:lindahl2017}. The researchers' explanation is an ordinary one. Long, still, eyes-closed practice resembles sensory deprivation {cite:lindahl2014}. Those meditators did not report teachers.

## Not ill

He went to a psychiatrist half hoping to be told he was sick, and was told to keep going: {q:01-prologue|The worst thing you could do is suppress this.}

Psychiatry has had a place for this since 1994, when its diagnostic manual added "religious or spiritual problem" as something a clinician may attend to that is not a disorder {cite:lukoff1992}. And a Yale study found people who hear voices every day and are not ill. What they hear resembles what patients hear. The difference is that they can start and stop it, are not distressed by it, and find the voices kind {cite:powers2017}. That is Steven's account of his own case nearly point for point: {q:07-telepathy|You have the ultimate control.} One difference is worth noting. The people in that study began hearing voices as children. He was in his mid-thirties.

## The ordinary reading

There is a reading of this whole chapter that needs no one on the other end, and he supplies half of it himself: {q:01-prologue|It feels like writing dialogue in a book a lot of the time}. He wrote fiction for years. In one study, 92 percent of fiction writers said their characters sometimes act on their own {cite:taylor2003}. An anthropologist who spent years with evangelical congregations describes how people learn to hear God through practice, including by pretending {cite:luhrmann2012}, and Steven says of his own start: {q:01-prologue|the more I entertain it, the more real it becomes}.

Neither study says the presences are not real. Both say that this is what sustained, serious attention to inner experience produces in healthy people, whether or not anyone is there.

## What I wonder

What the ordinary reading has to account for is how hard he pushed against his own experience. A person inventing companions does not usually go looking for a diagnosis first, or say he would love for it to be insanity. That is not evidence of what the companions were. It is evidence about him, and it is the reason I kept reading.
