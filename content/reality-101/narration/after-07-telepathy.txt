## A vocabulary with a history

Much of the language in this chapter was not new with him, and he says so. He credits "thought balls" to a teacher, Birdie Jaworski. The idea goes back at least to Robert Monroe, a radio executive who began having out-of-body experiences in 1958 and wrote three books about them. Monroe's second book has a glossary. It defines a "rote" as a thought ball, and an "ident" as a mental name or address, the energy pattern of a person or thing {cite:monroe1985}. That is Steven's soul tone. Monroe also describes classes during sleep, teachers of astonishing patience, and lessons delivered by simulation, and in his last book he concludes that his guide was part of his own larger self {cite:monroe1994}. Steven is told: {q:02-reality-101|Even your experience of me is you.}

So this is a lineage, passed through the teachers he trained with, and it should be counted as one. A shared vocabulary is not independent confirmation.

## People who never met his teachers

Two older sources describe the same mechanics from outside that lineage.

Emanuel Swedenborg, an eighteenth-century Swedish scientist who spent his last decades reporting conversations with spirits, wrote that they cannot speak to a person in their own language, only through that person's own words and memories {cite:swedenborg1758}. Steven: {q:07-telepathy|they're using your hardware and your reality.}

René Warcollier, a French chemical engineer, ran drawing-transmission trials from the 1920s and concluded that what arrives is fragmentary, is reshaped by the receiver's own mind, and comes most easily at the edge of sleep {cite:warcollier1948}. That is this chapter's account of distortion, and its advice about when to practice.

## What the laboratories found

The best-known telepathy experiments put a receiver in mild sensory isolation while a sender concentrates on a picture. Supporters' analyses of these keep finding a small excess, hits around 31 or 32 percent where chance is 25 {cite:bemhonorton1994,storm2010,tressoldistorm2024}. The main analysis run by skeptics found nothing {cite:miltonwiseman1999}. A 2018 review in a leading psychology journal argued the evidence is comparable to that for accepted effects, and the reply two years later argued it cannot be right because the thing is impossible {cite:cardena2018,reberalcock2020}. No result is accepted by mainstream science. Experiments on telepathy in dreams worked in one laboratory and failed when others tried {cite:belvederefoulkes1971}.

One small finding fits his account in an odd way. He says exact answers, like the solution to a math problem, are the hardest thing to get. A study of lucid dreamers found that the characters they meet in dreams are bad at arithmetic {cite:stumbrys2011}. You can read that as support for the view that the figures are made by the dreamer's own mind. You can also read it his way, that anything arriving has to come through the receiver.

## What is his

What I have not found anywhere else is the bookkeeping. People who write about channeling do not usually publish their own error rate. He does: clear, unmistakable contact about one time in fifty, and most of the rest, by his estimate, himself. He ranks his channels by how much he trusts them and puts words last.

He also refuses the grandest version of the claim. Others say telepathy leaves no room for misunderstanding. His answer: {q:07-telepathy|This is absolutely not true. Just regular old misunderstanding is still possible.}

## What I wonder

By his own numbers, a person following these instructions will mostly be talking to themselves, with no reliable way to know when they aren't. He does not hide that. He thinks the remainder is worth it.

I don't know if there is a remainder. I notice that the practice he describes, sitting quietly, bringing someone to mind with attention, saying what you have to say and being polite about it, would be worth doing for the dead even on the most skeptical account of where the answers come from.
